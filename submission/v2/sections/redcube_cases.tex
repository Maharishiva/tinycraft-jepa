\section{Three cases in the redcube world}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Case 1: a learned latent model can guide nearby goals}
\label{sec:case-one}

With deterministic dynamics and per-frame gray noise, the grid JEPA exposes cube position with
$0.115\pm0.012$ pixels of L1 error across three fits. We test whether its predictor and latent-goal
cost can use that state. Horizon-three MPC enumerates all 64 native action sequences, minimizes
summed squared distance to an encoded goal image, executes one action, and replans from the real
observation. It uses no state coordinates, position head, decoder output, or transformed latent.

The model guides nearby goals reliably (Figure~\ref{fig:goal-distance}). All three fits reach every
one- and two-action-distance target, and $94.5$--$96.9\%$ of three-action-distance targets, within
the common 24-action budget. Random actions reach $14.8\%$ of the latter; horizon-one latent MPC
reaches $12.5$--$17.2\%$. This establishes elementary goal-directed control through the learned
representation and predictor despite changing visual noise.

The operating range is limited. Success falls to $19.5$--$26.6\%$ at distance six, $0.8$--$2.3\%$
at distance nine, and zero at distance 12. The original mixed six-to-12-action test remains a weak
result: $5$, $5$, and $9$ hits out of 128, with minimum-distance means worse than random. The full
distance sweep was declared after that result and changes the goals, not the controller. It retains
all six distances, all three models, and the same 128 valid histories. Thus the positive result is
local goal-directed control, not long-horizon planning or a global guarantee about latent goal geometry.

\subsection{Case 2: nuisance and stochastic motion can hide the controlled object}
\label{sec:case-two}

Adding wind under the same per-frame gray background raises real-encoding position error to
$21.33\pm0.06$ pixels across the three grid models. Keeping the background fixed for an episode
instead gives $0.668\pm0.049$ pixels under wind (Table~\ref{tab:cube-main}). This reverses the deterministic comparison, where per-frame noise gives lower error than
persistent noise ($0.115$ versus $0.764$ pixels). The observed boundary is therefore a
noise-persistence $\times$ stochastic-motion interaction: persistent noise preserves accessible
state under wind in the same recipe that fails with fresh noise.

The failure already appears when reading real encoded observations, before recursive prediction
is evaluated. Reconstructions of those real encodings provide a complementary visual test of the
learned observation interface. A failed read-out does not prove that every possible decoder lacks
the state; it establishes a practical failure to expose position under the fixed diagnostic.
The per-frame/wind fits also retain high prediction loss, about $0.733$ per coordinate. They are
not examples of the zero-loss static-nuisance optimum from Section~\ref{sec:nuisance-example}.

\subsection{Case 3: stochastic forecasts and imagined control}
\label{sec:case-three}

Persistent-gray wind is a candidate for separating representation from stochastic prediction:
current position remains accessible, but future movement is uncertain. The delayed-reveal gate
adds a decision that must wait for new information. In these settings, we compare policies trained
inside JEPA and flow models through the same pixel-policy architecture, alongside real-simulator
controls using the same posterior-decoded observations. This comparison tests the learned
imagination packages; a claim that one model supports control requires the actual policy outcomes.
