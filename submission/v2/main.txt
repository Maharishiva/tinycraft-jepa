                                                  A BSTRACT
008
009
010
               Joint-embedding world models learn both which variables to retain and how to
               predict them. These choices can fail in different ways: predictable nuisance can
011
               displace useful state, and a correct mean future can omit information needed for a
012
               decision. We study these requirements in minimal redcube worlds, moving from
013            deterministic goal-directed motion to noisy observations and stochastic dynamics.
014            Two exact examples expose a limitation even under ideal prediction. A planner can
015            rank actions incorrectly when it evaluates squared goal distance at the mean rather
016            than averaging outcome costs; recursively applying an exact nonlinear conditional
017            mean can also miss the true multi-step mean. A known Gaussian nuisance con-
018            struction gives the corresponding representation-level failure. In a matched three-
019            seed study, the ranking of background persistence reverses with dynamics: per-
020            frame noise gives lower position error than persistent noise without wind (0.115
021
               versus 0.764 pixels), but much higher error with wind (21.33 versus 0.668). The
               same latent model supports nearby goal-directed movement, while the unchanged
022
               short-horizon controller degrades sharply on distant visual goals. Exact ﬁnite-
023
               branch calculations demonstrate small mean-only action-ranking changes in two
024            frozen encoders, without a demonstrated physical-cost beneﬁt. The central dis-
025            tinction is between retaining the state a task needs and retaining the predictive
026            information its decisions need.

029   1    I NTRODUCTION
030
031   In the tasks studied here, background texture changes neither the requested goal nor the object’s
032   dynamics or reward. A useful abstraction can ignore that texture while preserving where the object
033   is and what an action can do to it. Joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs) learn this
034   abstraction by predicting future representations rather than reconstructing every future pixel (LeCun,
035   2022; Assran et al., 2023; Bardes et al., 2024; Maes et al., 2026). The promise is attractive for
036   physical intelligence: learn a compact description of the world that can be reused across goals. The
037   difﬁculty is that predictive learning must decide both what to represent and what kind of prediction
038
      a decision needs.
039   These are separate requirements. First, an easy-to-predict feature need not be useful for control. A
040   background ﬁxed within an episode is perfectly predictable even if the controlled object is ignored
041   (Sobal et al., 2022). Second, retaining the right state does not make its mean future a sufﬁcient
042   description of uncertain outcomes. An action that lands equally often on either side of a target has
043   its mean exactly on the target. A planner that scores that mean can prefer it to an action that reliably
044
      lands close by. The mean prediction can be correct and the decision wrong.
045   We study both requirements in a minimal visual world: one red cube, four movement actions, a
046   background, and optional stochastic wind or a moving wall. The simplicity is deliberate. Cube
047   position, the requested goal, and the executed path are directly visible, so we can distinguish three
048   cases without relying on a large game’s aggregate score:
049
050
            1. Readable state and goal-directed movement. Deterministic movement with changing
               visual noise is the initial control condition. Requested visual goals and actual trajectories
051
               test planning beyond merely staying alive.
052
053         2. Failing to expose the controlled state. Varying background persistence and wind tests
               whether the same predictive-learning recipe retains an accessible description of the cube.


                                                         1

054
            3. Retaining state while predicting an uncertain future. Stochastic movement and a late-
055            revealed opening test the decisions made from a learned imagination model. Exact ﬁnite-
056            branch calculations separately isolate the effect of replacing a distribution by its mean.
057
058   Two elementary calculations make the third case precise. The ﬁrst separates the cost of a mean
059   from the expected cost of an outcome. The second shows that recursively applying an exact one-
060   step conditional mean need not predict a later mean. Both hold under ideal prediction, without
061
      an optimization failure. A Gaussian nuisance construction supplies the corresponding limit on the
      representation-learning objective. These mechanisms motivate the experiments; the experiments
062
      determine which failure is actually observed in each learned model.
063
064   The distinctive empirical result is a reversal in the effect of nuisance persistence. Under predictable
065   motion, changing gray noise yields more accurate position read-out than persistent noise; with fresh
066   wind innovations, changing noise fails while persistent noise preserves subpixel read-out. The archi-
067
      tecture, latent dimension, training data size, updates, and three-model replication are matched across
      this comparison. We use actual goal-directed paths and a shared-policy JEPA/ﬂow comparison to
068
      determine what this representation boundary means for behavior. The point is not to rediscover that
069
      prediction and control differ, but to identify when the same predictive-learning recipe changes which
070   physical state it exposes.

      2.1   A N EXACT MEAN CAN RANK ACTIONS INCORRECTLY
075
076   For a ﬁxed encoder, squared prediction loss is minimized by the conditional mean m(c, a) = E[Z ′ |
077   c, a], where c is the available history. A goal planner instead needs to compare outcome costs. For a
078   ﬁxed latent goal zg ,
079                                            
                           E ∥Z ′ − zg ∥2 | c, a = ∥m(c, a) − zg ∥2 + trCov(Z ′ | c, a).                (1)
080
081
      The ﬁrst term scores the mean; the second records the spread around it. An action-dependent spread
      can change which action minimizes expected cost.
082
083   Consider the exact scalar example in Figure 1. The goal is zero. Action A produces X ′ = −1 or
084   +1, each with probability 1/2; action B produces X ′ = 0.1 deterministically. The two means are 0
085   and 0.1, so their squared distances to the goal are 0 and 0.01: a mean-goal planner chooses A. The
086   correct expected costs are 1 and 0.01: B is better. No improved estimate of the same mean repairs
087
      this ranking. The omitted variance is exactly the missing quantity.
088

      2.2   A N EXACT ONE - STEP MEAN NEED NOT COMPOSE INTO A LATER MEAN
090
      Let Xt+1 = Xt2 +ξt , with independent fair noise ξt ∈ {−1, +1} and X0 = 0. The exact conditional
091   mean is m(x) = x2 , so repeated mean prediction remains zero. In the real process, X1 is −1 or 1
092   and X2 is 0 or 2, each fairly. Consequently, EX2 = 1 ̸= m(m(0)) = 0. More generally,

094
095   while recursive mean substitution retains only the ﬁrst term. This discrepancy occurs with an exact
096
      one-step predictor and ﬁnite-support noise.
097   These examples concern mean-state rollout and goal evaluation. A model that predicts expected
098   costs or suitable value functions directly can preserve the needed expectations without generating
099   samples (Wan et al., 2019; Grimm et al., 2021). A distributional world model offers another route: it
100   can retain distinct outcomes and average their costs. For the single quadratic goal cost in Equation 1,
101   the mean plus one scalar variance trace per action already sufﬁces. Whether a ﬁtted model achieves
102
      that beneﬁt is an empirical question.

      Joint representation learning changes both the target and the conditioning features in this expression.
133
      It can make prediction easy by retaining a feature that barely changes. Write an observation as
134   Xt = R(Pt , B), where Pt is controlled state and B is an irrelevant nuisance ﬁxed for the episode.
135   If the nuisance is recoverable and can be mapped to ϕ(B) ∼ N (0, I), an encoder that returns
136   only ϕ(B) is fully non-collapsed. Copying its last latent predicts the next one with zero error. A
137   Gaussian marginal regularizer is also zero at the population level. Thus a global optimum can ignore
138   the controlled state entirely.
139
      This is the predictable-nuisance mechanism of Sobal et al. (2022), specialized here to the Gaussian-
140   regularized objective. The construction assumes a sufﬁciently rich nuisance and a realizable encoder;
141   it is not an assertion that a ﬁnite trained network reaches that optimum. It explains why non-collapse
142   alone cannot choose the feature that matters. The empirical test is whether the learned representation
143   continues to expose cube state when nuisance and dynamics change. Full assumptions and proofs
144   are in Appendix B.

      3       M INIMAL WORLDS AND MODEL COMPARISON
147
148   One object, controlled changes. A red 6 × 6 cube moves in a 63 × 63 RGB frame. Four native
149   actions move it three pixels left, up, right, or down. Contact with the border terminates an episode
150   with reward −1; other rewards are zero, and the cap is 128 steps. Background noise is either re-
151   drawn each frame or held ﬁxed for the episode. Gray noise shares its sample across RGB channels.
152   Optional wind is a hidden two-dimensional random walk: each coordinate increments by −1, 0, +1
153   with probabilities 0.1, 0.8, 0.1, clipped to [−2, 2], and is added to the action displacement. This
154   produces fresh uncertainty even when previous motion is known.
155   The gate variant adds an approaching wall with a top or bottom opening sampled at spawn and
156   revealed only after a ﬁxed delay. Retreating gives time to observe the opening and respond. The
157   common solid part of the wall is present in both possible futures, so its disappearance from a decoded
158   forecast is an observed model/interface error, not the exact pixel average of the two gaps.
159
160   Joint-embedding prediction. The grid encoder produces zt = fθ (xt ) ∈ R512 , arranged as 4×4×

181   4     T HREE CASES IN THE REDCUBE WORLD
182
183   4.1   C ASE 1: A LEARNED LATENT MODEL CAN GUIDE NEARBY GOALS
184
185   With deterministic dynamics and per-frame gray noise, the grid JEPA exposes cube position with
186   0.115 ± 0.012 pixels of L1 error across three ﬁts. We test whether its predictor and latent-goal
187   cost can use that state. Horizon-three MPC enumerates all 64 native action sequences, minimizes
188   summed squared distance to an encoded goal image, executes one action, and replans from the real
189   observation. It uses no state coordinates, position head, decoder output, or transformed latent.
190   The model guides nearby goals reliably (Figure 2). All three ﬁts reach every one- and two-action-
191   distance target, and 94.5–96.9% of three-action-distance targets, within the common 24-action bud-
192   get. Random actions reach 14.8% of the latter; horizon-one latent MPC reaches 12.5–17.2%. This
193   establishes elementary goal-directed control through the learned representation and predictor despite
194   changing visual noise.
195   The operating range is limited. Success falls to 19.5–26.6% at distance six, 0.8–2.3% at distance
196   nine, and zero at distance 12. The original mixed six-to-12-action test remains a weak result: 5, 5,
197   and 9 hits out of 128, with minimum-distance means worse than random. The full distance sweep
198   was declared after that result and changes the goals, not the controller. It retains all six distances, all
199   three models, and the same 128 valid histories. Thus the positive result is local goal-directed control,
200   not long-horizon planning or a global guarantee about latent goal geometry.
201   The actual paths are selected by ﬁxed case index, rather than success. Appendix O retains the original
202   goal distribution, failures and complete secondary outcomes; Appendix O.1 gives the follow-up
203   counts and selection rules.

281   5    R ELATED WORK
282
283
284
      Joint-embedding prediction avoids reconstructing all observable detail (Assran et al., 2023; Bardes
      et al., 2024). LeWorldModel jointly learns its encoder and action-conditioned predictor with squared
285
      loss and Gaussian regularization (Maes et al., 2026; Balestriero & LeCun, 2025); this is our di-
286
      rect learning setting. DINO-WM and V-JEPA 2 instead use frozen pretrained features for action-
287   conditioned prediction, and PLDM jointly learns representations with additional regularizers (Zhou
288   et al., 2024; Assran et al., 2025; Sobal et al., 2025). Sobal et al. already study action-conditioned
289   moving objects with ﬁxed or changing distractors, encoded and predicted state probes, repeated ﬁts,
290   and a generative reference (Sobal et al., 2022). Our distinct comparison adds fresh unobserved mo-
291   tion innovations to the matched spatial recipe: the effect of noise persistence reverses under wind.
292   This is a boundary in a different recipe, not a contradiction of their ﬁndings. Recent contrastive work
293   also examines slow-noise bias (Gulati & Nemenman, 2026).
294   Prediction and planning need not favor the same geometry. LeWorldModel reports accurate probes
295   alongside weaker planning; Temporal-Distance JEPA and RC-aux introduce temporal or reachability
296   constraints, while physical-state grounding adds task-state supervision (Maes et al., 2026; Bai &
297   Xiong, 2026; Li et al., 2026; Yan et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026). Terver et al. (2026) study architectural
298   and planner choices and discuss multimodal conditional means. Our exact examples focus on the
299   decision consequences of evaluating costs at a mean and recursively composing mean transitions.
300   FlowWM learns stochastic feature-space futures and evaluates mode coverage and future perception
301   with a frozen visual encoder (Porcher et al., 2026). It is not action-conditioned and reports no
302   control experiments. Our action-conditioned JEPA/ﬂow comparison instead studies jointly learned
303   state and real behavior. Generative imagination models have demonstrated useful control (Hafner
304   et al., 2020; Alonso et al., 2024; Hafner et al., 2025), while also exhibiting failures of perception,
305   action conditioning, and rollout (Hansen & Wang, 2026).
306   The broader prediction–control mismatch is longstanding (Lambert et al., 2020). Value-aware learn-
307   ing, expectation models, and value equivalence model the quantities needed by a particular decision
308   class (Farahmand et al., 2017; Wan et al., 2019; Grimm et al., 2020; 2021). Temporal-difference
309   JEPA predicts policy-conditioned successor features for value-based control (Bagatella et al., 2026).
310   These approaches explain why our mean-state counterexamples are not impossibility results for all
311   deterministic world models.

      6    D ISCUSSION
315
316   The minimal worlds separate two demands on an abstract world model. Its representation must
317   retain the variables on which a decision depends. Its prediction must preserve the expectations that
318   the planner will evaluate. A non-collapsed representation can fail the ﬁrst demand by concentrating
319   on predictable nuisance. A correct conditional-mean model can fail the second when a planner
320
      applies nonlinear costs or composes nonlinear dynamics at that mean.
321   The redcube comparisons make these demands visible at different stages: requested-goal movement,
322   position accessible in real observations, and behavior learned from imagined futures. The matched
323   nuisance study already shows a reproducible change in accessible state under combined fresh noise
      and wind. Its high prediction loss separates the measured ﬁnite-budget failure from the idealized


                                                          6
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2027




324
      nuisance optimum. Likewise, a practical JEPA/ﬂow control gap would compare complete learned
325   packages; the exact variance calculation is the narrower test that isolates point compression.
326
327   The empirical scope is small visual domains, speciﬁed models and training budgets, and the reported
328
      checkpoint and policy replications. The elementary examples establish limitations of mean-state
      planning, not a requirement that every useful world model generate samples. Expected-cost or
329
      value-equivalent models can preserve the needed decision quantities directly. General-purpose world
330
      models need a principle for choosing both the state to preserve and the predictive information that
331   their intended decisions require.

      A     W HAT THE OBJECTIVE DOES NOT GUARANTEE
487
488
      The experiments concern ﬁtted ﬁnite networks. The following population statements ask a narrower
489
      question: which guarantees follow from prediction error and a Gaussian latent marginal alone? They
490   do not characterize optimization or identify the cause of the empirical failures.
491
492
493
      A.1    N ON - COLLAPSE DOES NOT SELECT THE CONTROLLED STATE
494
      Write observations as xt = R(pt , b), where pt is controlled state and b is an episode-constant nui-
495
      sance recoverable from the image. Assume that b is independent of states and actions in the training
496   data. At deployment, assume it is independent of the initial state and transition randomness, and does
497   not directly affect dynamics or reward. Let SIGReg∞ be the nonnegative population characteristic-
498   function discrepancy, which vanishes for N (0, ID ) (Appendix B).
499
      Proposition 1 (Gaussian nuisance optimum; extending Sobal et al. (2022)). If the encoder class
500
      contains a map f (R(p, b)) = φ(b) with φ(b) ∼ N (0, ID ) and the predictor class contains the last-
501   latent copy map, their population prediction loss and SIGReg∞ are both zero. This is a global opti-
502   mum with covariance ID and I(zt ; p1:T ) = 0 under the training distribution. A deployed controller
503   observing only these latents, time, and its own actions has the value of a randomized state-blind
504   policy.
505
506   The argument is simple: nuisance changes across episodes, satisfying the marginal constraint, but
507   is constant within each episode, making prediction exact. Its deployment value follows by giving
508   a state-blind policy the same private nuisance draw and controller randomness. The Gaussianiza-
509
      tion assumption requires a rich nuisance and a realizable measurable map; a ﬁnite texture does not
      literally instantiate it. The ﬁnite-batch regularizer also differs from the population functional. A com-
510
      plementary innovation bound in Appendix B shows that an encoder preserving all state distances at
511
      a ﬁxed scale must incur prediction error for unpredictable state innovations.
512
513
514   A.2    D ECODABILITY DOES NOT SET A USEFUL PREDICTION SCALE
515
      Even preserving all state information leaves its geometry unconstrained. For any ε > 0, we can
516
      place the two values of a relevant bit less than 2ε apart while keeping the entire latent distribution
517
      exactly standard Gaussian.
518
519   Proposition 2 (Exact information at arbitrarily small scale). For every ε > 0 and D ≥ 1, there
520
      is a measurable observation process with episode-constant nuisance b and a fresh independent fair
      state bit pt , and an encoder fε , such that zt ∼ N (0, ID ) and both pt and b are exactly recoverable
521
      almost surely. For ﬁxed b, 0 < ∥fε (R(1, b)) − fε (R(0, b))∥ < 2ε. The optimal next-latent predictor
522
      is independent of the next bit and has coordinate-mean squared error in (0, ε2 /D]. With reward
523   1{at = pt }, latent feedback attains value 1 per step, while every state-blind policy attains 1/2.
524
525   The construction partitions one Gaussian coordinate into narrow intervals and uses the bit to select
526   the lower or upper half of each interval’s conditional probability mass. The nuisance ﬁxes the
527   interval and the within-half quantile. This preserves the Gaussian marginal and exact decoding while
528   making the two states arbitrarily close. The full proof is in Appendix B. It uses inﬁnite-precision
529   measurable coding; the next bit is intrinsically unpredictable. It therefore separates information from
530   scale, without proving avoidable prediction error or explaining deletion of the predictable gate wall.
531
532   Why scale matters for a downstream read-out. For any ﬁxed L-Lipschitz downstream score q
533   (for example, an action-value read-out) and any coupled real and predicted next latents,
534                                                        q
535                                 Eq(Z ′ ) − Eq(Zb′ ) ≤ L E∥Z ′ − Zb′ ∥2 .                   (4)
536
537   This standard bound follows from Lipschitz continuity and Cauchy–Schwarz. Prediction error con-
538   trols score error only relative to the read-out’s sensitivity. Exact decodability alone supplies no
539   bound on that sensitivity. We do not estimate L or claim a control guarantee for the ﬁtted networks;
      the inequality makes explicit the missing bridge between a geometric loss and a decision.
