011                                               A BSTRACT
012
013            A failed policy trained in a world model can reﬂect errors in the model, its task
014            heads, or the controller-training procedure. We examine these alternatives in a
015
               jointly trained, squared-loss JEPA with a ﬁxed 512-dimensional representation.
               In Craftax, one frozen model reaches 93.2% of 384 nearby goals with six open-
016
               loop actions and 96.1% with replanning, versus 35.7% for no-op; success remains
017
               89.9% and 93.9% on displaced goals. A controlled moving-wall task then tests
018            whether poor imagined control identiﬁes a model failure. Across 45 matched PPO
019            runs, replacing learned transitions, rewards, and terminations with exact simulator
020            outcomes improves walls passed by 0.12–0.22 per episode across three model ﬁts,
021            but none of the 15 exact-environment policies exceeds 50% survival. Their mean
022            survival remains below 1.5% for every model. The common controller is itself
023            unsuccessful, so the earlier unmatched real-versus-imagined gap does not estab-
024            lish a task-solving deﬁcit caused by learned transitions. A replicated nuisance–
025            dynamics study supplies a separate representation-accessibility failure, while pop-
026
               ulation constructions clarify the limits of non-collapse guarantees. Together, the
               measurements distinguish working local planning, unsuccessful policy training,
027
               and inaccessible state under a stated learning recipe.

030   1    I NTRODUCTION
031
032   A world model can ignore texture while preserving a doorway’s position, available opening, and
033   response to action. Learning such abstractions without a downstream reward is a central attraction
034   of joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs): their targets are representations of future ob-
035   servations rather than future pixels (LeCun, 2022; Assran et al., 2023; Bardes et al., 2024). Recent
036   systems demonstrate useful goal-conditioned planning from both pretrained and jointly learned rep-
037   resentations (Assran et al., 2025; Sobal et al., 2025; Maes et al., 2026). The difﬁcult question is what
038
      a particular success or failure establishes about the learned model.
039   We study this question through interventions on a jointly trained squared-loss JEPA. The model has a
040   ﬁxed 512-dimensional latent and a Gaussian marginal regularizer. The environments progress from
041   Craftax to a moving-wall task and then a single cube with controlled nuisance and dynamics. Each
042   step removes complexity to make a narrower question testable: can the model guide local actions,
043   can its training environment support a competent controller, and under which conditions does its
044
      representation expose object position?
045   Craftax supplies a positive result with useful controls. On 384 fresh episodes, nearby position plan-
046   ning succeeds in 93.2% of open-loop trials and 96.1% with replanning. No-op succeeds in 35.7%,
047   because some targets share the starting position; on the 247 displaced targets, planned success re-
048   mains 89.9% and 93.9%. Goal generation and execution use independent randomness. These mea-
049   surements establish local action-selection capability for the existing checkpoint.
050   The gate gives a different result. Earlier policies trained in imagination performed worse than poli-
051   cies trained on real transitions, and decoded predictions lost a visible wall. Those observations
052   suggested a model limitation, but also changed the task heads and PPO protocol. We therefore train
053   45 policies under one matched protocol, crossing three frozen models and ﬁve policy seeds with
      learned transitions, exact transitions with ﬁtted heads, and exact transitions with true signals. Even

057   changes the interpretation of the original imagination result.

067   policy replication make the evidence inspectable. The conclusions concern this learning recipe and
068   its tested interfaces; closely related predictive-learning methods are discussed in Section 7.

139
140
      A task with a delayed observation. A vertical wall approaches a controlled cube in a 63 × 63
141
      image. Its gap is sampled at spawn, at the top or bottom with equal probability, and becomes
142   visible at a ﬁxed reveal point. From the centre, either gap needs ﬁve vertical moves but is revealed
143   only two steps before contact; retreating creates time to respond. Wall or border contact terminates

146   evaluation archive.
147
      The gate supports two complementary interventions on the same frozen models. First, we reﬁt a
148   read-out on forecast latents to test transfer from encoded observations. Second, we replace learned
149   training transitions and task signals with exact simulator outcomes under a common PPO protocol.
150   These interventions respectively test access to forecast state and the controller-training baseline.

175   4.2   T HE EXACT- ENVIRONMENT BASELINE FAILS TO SOLVE THE GATE
176
177   None of the 15 policies trained with exact transitions and true task signals reaches 50% survival.
178   Their mean survival is 0.47%, 0.39%, and 1.41% for model seeds 0, 1, and 2 (Figure 2; Table 13 in
179   the appendix). The same controller is therefore unsuccessful even when transition, reward, and ter-
180   mination prediction errors are removed from its training environment. The poor policy performance
181
      in imagination therefore cannot be attributed to the learned transition model alone.
182
183   A ﬁxed training protocol. For every frozen model, we train ﬁve recurrent MLP-PPO policies
184   (Schulman et al., 2017) in each of three conditions: learned transitions with ﬁtted reward/termination
185
      heads; exact simulator transitions re-encoded by the same encoder with the same ﬁtted heads; and
      exact re-encoded transitions with true rewards and terminations. Actor inputs contain no simulator
186
      state. The 45 runs share their start-state mixture, context burn-in, rollout length, optimizer, updates,
187
      and evaluation keys within model/policy-seed pairs. Each completes 150 updates of 512 × 16 transi-
188   tions and is evaluated at its ﬁnal checkpoint on 256 real episodes. Walls passed counts exits before
189   or on the terminal transition, including an exit on the same step as death. Survival requires remain-
190   ing alive through step 128; these are the two primary outcomes; survival above 50% is a secondary
191   operational threshold.
192
      Exact-simulator trajectories become absorbing after true termination. With ﬁtted heads, learned
193
      continuation may still weight those terminal states. We report this support change alongside the
194   transition intervention. Policy-seed intervals are conditional on a ﬁxed model and its evaluation
195   set; the three model effects remain separate. Appendix K records the pairing checks, uncertainty

      4.3   T RANSITION AND TASK - SIGNAL SUBSTITUTIONS HAVE DIFFERENT EFFECTS
204
205   Keeping the ﬁtted heads while substituting exact transitions lowers mean walls passed to 0.230,
206   0.068, and 0.096. Replacing those heads with true signals then raises the means to 0.452, 0.341, and
207   0.543. These contrasts expose sensitivity to how transitions and task signals are combined. They
208   do not establish that less accurate dynamics are intrinsically better: the ﬁtted-head condition applies
209   its learned continuation after the simulator has entered an absorbing terminal state. Its logged post-
210   terminal weight fraction averages 12.3%, 11.2%, and 9.4% across updates and policy seeds in the
211   three models.
212   Real-transition head diagnostics already show a weak wall-collision signal. For model 0, ﬁtted
213   reward is about −0.05 on wall deaths and −0.89 on border deaths, against the true −1; termination
214   AUC is 0.65 versus 0.96. Yet correcting both dynamics and signals still leaves the common MLP-
215   PPO recipe unsuccessful. The exact condition leaves the actor’s access to encoded state and its
      training procedure as unresolved limitations.

306   5                       R EMOVE THE WALL : NUISANCE AND DYNAMICS INTERACT
307
308   Cube worlds reduce the scene to one moving object. Random backgrounds either change indepen-
309   dently each frame or remain ﬁxed for an episode; dynamics are deterministic or include hidden wind.
310   A ﬁxed background is predictable but irrelevant to position. Per-frame noise is unpredictable and
311   also irrelevant. These cases distinguish visual complexity from predictive usefulness.
312   The strongest replicated comparison holds the 512-dimensional grid architecture, dataset size
313   (500,000 transitions), and training length (8,500 updates) ﬁxed across four gray-noise conditions.
314   A supervised nonlinear position read-out is ﬁtted on training encodings and evaluated on indepen-
315   dent evaluation frames. It achieves subpixel error whenever either the background is persistent or
316   dynamics are deterministic. Combining per-frame noise with wind raises error to 21.33 ± 0.06
317   pixels across three model initializations (Table 1). This is a failure to expose position through the
318   tested read-out. Its prediction loss remains high, about 0.73 per coordinate, so it is not evidence of
319   a successfully optimized low-loss nuisance solution.
320   Other ﬁtted models fail at different interfaces. On a black background without wind, the class-token
321   JEPA permits 0.087-pixel position read-out from real encodings, but that read-out has 45.27-pixel
322   error on eight-step predictions. A ﬂow model also fails on persistent color texture, with 18.56-pixel
323   real-latent error. These are single-ﬁt observations with different dataset sizes, reported separately in
      Appendix C; they preclude a uniform ranking of model families. Together, the cube results motivate

      6    N ON - COLLAPSE LEAVES FEATURE CHOICE OPEN
329
330
331   A Gaussian marginal can describe either useful state or an episode-speciﬁc nuisance. The known
332   static-nuisance construction of Sobal et al. (2022) makes this explicit: an encoder can Gaussianize
333
      an irrelevant constant texture and a predictor can copy it, obtaining zero population prediction loss
      and zero Gaussian discrepancy. We state its control consequence and assumptions in Appendix A.
334
      This is a boundary of the objective’s guarantees, not an identiﬁed explanation of the high-loss gray-
335
      frame/wind ﬁts.
336
337   A second construction separates information from geometric scale. An exactly Gaussian latent can
338   encode a control-relevant bit invertibly while placing its two values arbitrarily close together. For an
339
      L-Lipschitz downstream score, latent root-mean-squared prediction error bounds score error only
      after multiplication by L; exact decodability supplies no bound on that sensitivity. The construction
340
      uses unrestricted measurable coding and a fresh unpredictable bit. Its full statement and proof clarify
341
      this narrow scope, without turning a failed neural read-out into a proof that information has been
342   deleted.

      7    R ELATED WORK
346
347
348   Which JEPA setting? Image and video JEPAs learn by predicting representations (Assran et al.,
349
      2023; Bardes et al., 2024). DINO-WM ﬁts dynamics on frozen visual features, and V-JEPA 2 freezes
      its encoder for action-conditioned prediction with an L1 objective (Zhou et al., 2024; Assran et al.,
350
      2025). PLDM instead jointly learns its encoder and dynamics with multiple regularizers (Sobal et al.,
351
      2025). Our direct setting is LeWorldModel’s jointly trained squared-loss predictor with SIGReg
352   (Maes et al., 2026; Balestriero & LeCun, 2025); the population statements do not automatically
353   transfer to these other objectives.
354
355
356   Predictability, geometry, and planning. Predictable nuisance is an established limitation of joint
357   embeddings (Sobal et al., 2022). Recent work studies how contrastive sampling changes slow-noise
358   bias (Gulati & Nemenman, 2026), and how temporal-distance objectives improve planning-relevant
359
      geometry (Bai & Xiong, 2026). LeWorldModel already reports accurate probes alongside weaker
      planning, while Terver et al. (2026) examine architecture and planner choices and discuss multi-
360
      modal conditional means. Self-predictive abstraction analyses similarly distinguish predictive struc-
361
      ture from task sufﬁciency (Tang et al., 2023; Ni et al., 2024). Physical-state grounding also directly
362   tests recoverability and goal-conditioned control, using privileged state or proprioceptive supervi-
363   sion beyond a prediction-only objective (Yan et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026). These precedents moti-
364   vate a controlled intervention in a speciﬁed learning recipe, rather than a ﬁrst claim that prediction
365   and control can disagree.
366
367
368   Models for decisions. Prediction–control mismatch predates JEPAs (Lambert et al., 2020). Value-
369
      aware model learning, value equivalence, and proper value equivalence ask which errors affect down-
      stream Bellman computations and policy values (Farahmand et al., 2017; Grimm et al., 2020; 2021).
370
      Expectation models can sufﬁce for suitable value-function classes (Wan et al., 2019); conditional-
371
      mean prediction is therefore not inherently incompatible with control. Stochastic imagination mod-
372   els provide another route (Hafner et al., 2020; Alonso et al., 2024; Hafner et al., 2025), but they
373   too can fail at encoding, action conditioning, or rollout stages (Hansen & Wang, 2026). Temporal-
374   difference JEPA instead predicts policy-conditioned successor features with detached TD targets
375   and EMA target networks, allowing value-based control in stochastic MDPs (Bagatella et al., 2026).
376   RC-aux retains target gradients and adds multi-horizon prediction and reachability supervision in
377   a deterministic-dynamics analysis (Li et al., 2026). These objectives provide stronger or different
      constraints than one-step squared prediction with a marginal prior.

      8   D ISCUSSION
379
380
      The most consequential gate result is the unsuccessful exact-environment control. Removing learned
381   transition and task-signal errors improves wall counts, yet every ﬁtted model remains below 1.5%
382   mean survival with the common MLP-PPO recipe. An explanation based only on poor world-model
383   dynamics is therefore incomplete. The earlier decoder/CNN result establishes that at least one frozen
384   encoder contains useful task information under a different read-out and training procedure; it does
385   not supply the missing successful baseline for this matched controller.
386
      Craftax and the nuisance cubes deﬁne complementary boundaries. The existing JEPA guides local
387   position-reaching actions beyond a no-op baseline under independent execution noise. The same
388   model family has a reproducible position-readout failure in the gray-frame/wind cube condition.
389   Together, these observations support evaluating a proposed model improvement at the interface it ac-
390   tually changes: representation accessibility, forecast read-out, ﬁtted task signals, or policy training.

      The main limitations are model replication and identiﬁcation. Craftax uses one checkpoint; the gate
392

595
596
      The experiments concern ﬁtted ﬁnite networks. The following population statements ask a narrower
597
      question: which guarantees follow from prediction error and a Gaussian latent marginal alone? They
598   do not characterize optimization or identify the cause of the empirical failures.

      A.1    N ON - COLLAPSE DOES NOT SELECT THE CONTROLLED STATE
602
      Write observations as xt = R(pt , b), where pt is controlled state and b is an episode-constant nui-
603
      sance recoverable from the image. Assume that b is independent of states and actions in the training
604   data. At deployment, assume it is independent of the initial state and transition randomness, and does
605   not directly affect dynamics or reward. Let SIGReg∞ be the nonnegative population characteristic-
606   function discrepancy, which vanishes for N (0, ID ) (Appendix B).
607
      Proposition 1 (Gaussian nuisance optimum; extending Sobal et al. (2022)). If the encoder class
608
      contains a map f (R(p, b)) = φ(b) with φ(b) ∼ N (0, ID ) and the predictor class contains the last-
609   latent copy map, their population prediction loss and SIGReg∞ are both zero. This is a global opti-
610   mum with covariance ID and I(zt ; p1:T ) = 0 under the training distribution. A deployed controller
611   observing only these latents, time, and its own actions has the value of a randomized state-blind
612   policy.
613
614   The argument is simple: nuisance changes across episodes, satisfying the marginal constraint, but
615   is constant within each episode, making prediction exact. Its deployment value follows by giving
616   a state-blind policy the same private nuisance draw and controller randomness. The Gaussianiza-
617
      tion assumption requires a rich nuisance and a realizable measurable map; a ﬁnite texture does not
      literally instantiate it. The ﬁnite-batch regularizer also differs from the population functional. A com-
618
      plementary innovation bound in Appendix B shows that an encoder preserving all state distances at
619
      a ﬁxed scale must incur prediction error for unpredictable state innovations.

622   A.2    D ECODABILITY DOES NOT SET A USEFUL PREDICTION SCALE
623
      Even preserving all state information leaves its geometry unconstrained. For any ε > 0, we can
624
      place the two values of a relevant bit less than 2ε apart while keeping the entire latent distribution
625
      exactly standard Gaussian.
626
627   Proposition 2 (Exact information at arbitrarily small scale). For every ε > 0 and D ≥ 1, there
628
      is a measurable observation process with episode-constant nuisance b and a fresh independent fair

629
      almost surely. For ﬁxed b, 0 < ∥fε (R(1, b)) − fε (R(0, b))∥ < 2ε. The optimal next-latent predictor
630

631   1{at = pt }, latent feedback attains value 1 per step, while every state-blind policy attains 1/2.

787   Four cardinal actions move the object three pixels. Reset positions are uniformly sampled from
