011                                              A BSTRACT
012
013            A world model should discard visual detail without discarding the distinctions an
014            agent needs to act. Joint-embedding prediction makes this trade-off implicit: it
015            learns both the representation and the prediction target. We examine three sepa-
016            rate questions: what information remains encoded, what survives prediction, and
017            what supports control. We begin with Craftax, where one 512-dimensional joint-
018            embedding model reaches 121 of 128 nearby goals in a six-action open-loop test,
019
               yet its tested reward-planning pipeline underperforms random actions. A con-
               trolled moving-wall task then separates representation from imagination: decoded
020
               real encodings retain the wall, and policies using them exceed 50% survival in
021
               two of nine runs; decoded predicted rollouts lose the wall, and none of 27 policies
022            trained in the tested imagination pipeline reaches that threshold. These pipeline
023            outcomes include ﬁtted heads and policy optimization and do not isolate dynam-
024            ics as the cause. Finally, nuisance-texture worlds connect the observations to the
025            objective. We formalize a known predictable-nuisance failure under Gaussian
026            non-collapse, and construct an exactly Gaussian, fully decodable representation
027            in which a control-relevant bit has arbitrarily small prediction-loss scale. The
028            resulting lesson is a concrete evaluation requirement: retention, prediction, and
029            control must be checked separately.

032   1   I NTRODUCTION
033
034
      A useful world model need not reproduce every pixel. It must preserve the distinctions that change
035
      what an agent should do. For a robot approaching a doorway, the texture of the wall may be ir-
036   relevant; the wall’s position and the available opening are not. Learning this separation without
037   specifying a particular downstream reward is a central attraction of joint-embedding predictive ar-
038   chitectures (JEPAs): predict an informative representation of the future rather than reconstruct the
039   future image (LeCun, 2022; Assran et al., 2023; Bardes et al., 2024).
040
      Recent systems make this approach increasingly practical. Pretrained video representations support
041
      action-conditioned planning, and end-to-end objectives jointly learn representations and dynamics
042   (Assran et al., 2025; Sobal et al., 2025; Maes et al., 2026). These successes raise an evaluation
043   question: when a learned world model supports one behavior, what has that established about its
044   usefulness for another? Nearby goal reaching, recognizable decoded rollouts, and reward-driven
045   control measure different capabilities. A representation can encode a feature that its predictor does
046   not preserve; a predictor can achieve low error on features that a controller cannot use.
047
      We study these distinctions in a squared-loss JEPA with Gaussian marginal regularization, following
048   the end-to-end formulation of LeWorldModel (Maes et al., 2026; Balestriero & LeCun, 2025). The
049   study moves from a complex environment to progressively simpler ones (Figure 1). Craftax ﬁrst
050   exposes a split between short-horizon goal reaching and reward control. A moving-wall task reduces
051   the decision to one observable obstacle and an initially hidden gap. Cube worlds then remove
052   the obstacle and vary only visual nuisance and dynamics. The simpliﬁcation makes the question
053   sharper: is relevant information absent from the encoding, lost during prediction, or ineffective in
      the complete control pipeline?

142
143   Craftax-Classic combines movement, resources, inventory, and survival (Matthews et al., 2024). It
144
      is a useful starting point because reaching a nearby visual target and selecting a rewarding sequence
      of interactions can require different information. We evaluate one ﬁtted 512-dimensional grid JEPA
145
      in two protocols, both using real simulator outcomes.
146
147
148   Short-horizon goal reaching. After seven random warm-up actions, six additional random ac-
149
      tions generate a reachable goal image. The planner minimizes distance between its predicted end-
      point latent and the encoded goal. It then executes the selected six actions open loop, without
150
      replanning. Success means matching the goal’s ﬁnal player position; it does not require matching
151
      inventory or the entire scene. The planner reaches 121 of 128 goals, compared with 24 for random
152   actions (Table 1). Goal generation and execution share simulator randomness. This is evidence for
153   nearby goal reaching under the stated protocol, not long-horizon task completion.
154
155
      Reward-driven planning. We separately ﬁt reward and value heads on frozen encodings and eval-
156   uate 100-step behavior from 128 starts. The six-step CEM controller replans after every real obser-
157   vation. Its mean return is 0.47, below 1.45 for uniform random actions. A myopic reward controller,
158   which does not use predicted transitions, also underperforms random actions. The reward head’s
159   correlation falls from 0.92 on its ﬁtting data to 0.35 on held-out time rows, and value correlation
160   falls from 0.99 to 0.27. Poor performance therefore already appears in reward-based action selec-
161   tion without predicted transitions; the multi-step result concerns the combined head, dynamics, and
      planning pipeline.

193
      Decoded real encodings visibly retain the approaching wall (Figure 2). To test whether that infor-
194
      mation can support behavior, we train a convolutional policy in the real gate on the frozen decoder’s
195   output. Two of nine runs discover the survival strategy, both using encoder seed 1; the corresponding

197   at least one ﬁtted encoder under the decoder-and-CNN read-out. The decoder adds reconstruction
198   supervision outside the world-model budget, so this is a test of recoverable information rather than
199   unsupervised control sufﬁciency.
200

201   latents yield 0.94, with no discoveries in either group. An MLP on decoded latents similarly yields
202   0.92 walls. Thus failure of this MLP training recipe is not evidence that the encoder has lost the
203   obstacle. These comparisons do not set a capacity limit for all MLP policies; they locate a limitation
204   of the tested read-out and optimization procedure.

      The experiments concern ﬁtted ﬁnite networks. The following population statements ask a narrower

      6.1                                      N ON - COLLAPSE DOES NOT SELECT THE CONTROLLED STATE
369
370   Write observations as xt = R(pt , b), where pt is controlled state and b is an episode-constant nui-
371   sance recoverable from the image. Assume that b is independent of states and actions in the training
372   data. At deployment, assume it is independent of the initial state and transition randomness, and does
373   not directly affect dynamics or reward. Let SIGReg∞ be the nonnegative population characteristic-
374   function discrepancy, which vanishes for N (0, ID ) (Appendix A).
375   Proposition 1 (Gaussian nuisance optimum; extending Sobal et al. (2022)). If the encoder class
376   contains a map f (R(p, b)) = φ(b) with φ(b) ∼ N (0, ID ) and the predictor class contains the last-
377   latent copy map, their population prediction loss and SIGReg∞ are both zero. This is a global opti-
      mum with covariance ID and I(zt ; p1:T ) = 0 under the training distribution. A deployed controller


                                                                                               7

378
      observing only these latents, time, and its own actions has the value of a randomized state-blind
379   policy.
380
381   The argument is simple: nuisance changes across episodes, satisfying the marginal constraint, but
382   is constant within each episode, making prediction exact. Its deployment value follows by giving
383   a state-blind policy the same private nuisance draw and controller randomness. The Gaussianiza-
384   tion assumption requires a rich nuisance and a realizable measurable map; a ﬁnite texture does not
385
      literally instantiate it. The ﬁnite-batch regularizer also differs from the population functional. A com-
      plementary innovation bound in Appendix A shows that an encoder preserving all state distances at
386
      a ﬁxed scale must incur prediction error for unpredictable state innovations.

      6.2   D ECODABILITY DOES NOT SET A USEFUL PREDICTION SCALE
389
390   Even preserving all state information leaves its geometry unconstrained. For any ε > 0, we can
391   place the two values of a relevant bit less than 2ε apart while keeping the entire latent distribution
392   exactly standard Gaussian.
393   Proposition 2 (Exact information at arbitrarily small scale). For every ε > 0 and D ≥ 1, there
394   is a measurable observation process with episode-constant nuisance b and a fresh independent fair
395   state bit pt , and an encoder fε , such that zt ∼ N (0, ID ) and both pt and b are exactly recoverable
396   almost surely. For ﬁxed b, 0 < ∥fε (R(1, b)) − fε (R(0, b))∥ < 2ε. The optimal next-latent predictor
397   is independent of the next bit and has coordinate-mean squared error in (0, ε2 /D]. With reward
398   1{at = pt }, latent feedback attains value 1 per step, while every state-blind policy attains 1/2.
399
      The construction partitions one Gaussian coordinate into narrow intervals and uses the bit to select
400   the lower or upper half of each interval’s conditional probability mass. The nuisance ﬁxes the
401   interval and the within-half quantile. This preserves the Gaussian marginal and exact decoding while
402   making the two states arbitrarily close. The full proof is in Appendix A. It uses inﬁnite-precision
403   measurable coding; the next bit is intrinsically unpredictable. It therefore separates information from
404   scale, without proving avoidable prediction error or explaining deletion of the predictable gate wall.
405
406   Why scale matters for a downstream read-out. For a ﬁxed L-Lipschitz value read-out V and
407   any coupled real and predicted next latents,

417   Which JEPA setting? Image and video JEPAs learn by predicting representations (Assran et al.,
418   2023; Bardes et al., 2024). DINO-WM ﬁts dynamics on frozen visual features, and V-JEPA 2 freezes
419   its encoder for action-conditioned prediction with an L1 objective (Zhou et al., 2024; Assran et al.,
420   2025). PLDM instead jointly learns its encoder and dynamics with multiple regularizers (Sobal et al.,
421
      2025). Our direct setting is LeWorldModel’s jointly trained squared-loss predictor with SIGReg
      (Maes et al., 2026; Balestriero & LeCun, 2025); the population statements do not automatically
422
      transfer to these other objectives.
423
424
      Predictability, geometry, and planning. Predictable nuisance is an established limitation of joint
425
      embeddings (Sobal et al., 2022). Recent work studies how contrastive sampling changes slow-noise
426   bias (Gulati & Nemenman, 2026), and how temporal-distance objectives improve planning-relevant
427   geometry (Bai & Xiong, 2026). LeWorldModel already reports accurate probes alongside weaker
428   planning, while Terver et al. (2026) examine architecture and planner choices and discuss multi-
429   modal conditional means. Self-predictive abstraction analyses similarly distinguish predictive struc-
430   ture from task sufﬁciency (Tang et al., 2023; Ni et al., 2024). Our contribution is a controlled set
431   of representation, rollout, and policy diagnostics with explicit replication, together with a narrow
      separation between Gaussianity, exact decodability, and loss scale.

432
      Models for decisions. Prediction–control mismatch predates JEPAs (Lambert et al., 2020). Value-
433   aware model learning, value equivalence, and proper value equivalence ask which errors affect down-
434   stream Bellman computations and policy values (Farahmand et al., 2017; Grimm et al., 2020; 2021).
435   Expectation models can sufﬁce for suitable value-function classes (Wan et al., 2019); conditional-
436   mean prediction is therefore not inherently incompatible with control. Stochastic imagination mod-
437   els provide another route (Hafner et al., 2020; Alonso et al., 2024; Hafner et al., 2025), but they
438   too can fail at encoding, action conditioning, or rollout stages (Hansen & Wang, 2026). The same
439   interface-level scrutiny should apply to both families.
440
441   8   D ISCUSSION
442
443   The experiments separate capabilities that are often presented together. The Craftax checkpoint
444   supports nearby visual-goal planning, while its reward-planning pipeline has weak held-out reward
445   and value predictions. The gate encoders retain useful obstacle information under an added decoder
446   and CNN, while their tested imagination pipelines fail to discover the strategy. Nuisance cubes and
447   the population constructions show why a marginal non-collapse condition alone cannot settle either
448   question.
449   The practical consequence is to evaluate a world model at each interface: information recoverable
450   from real encodings, prediction of controlled counterfactuals, head generalization, and behavior
451   in the real environment. A controller’s failure should lead to this decomposition before it is used
452   to judge the representation; a successful probe should be followed by prediction and control tests
453   before it is used to certify imagination.
454   Our empirical scope is one jointly trained squared-loss objective, small visual domains, and short
455   training budgets. Craftax and the ﬂow reference each use one ﬁtted model. Gate predictors, heads,
456   and PPO protocols have not been isolated by a fully matched intervention. The theory gives popu-
457   lation existence results, with measurable constructions rather than a ﬁnite-network or optimization
458   analysis. In particular, the fresh-bit example does not explain loss of a predictable wall. What the
459   evidence does establish is that non-collapse, decodability, and useful imagined control are separate
460   properties, requiring separate support.

890   Every gate-policy endpoint uses the ﬁnal checkpoint and 256 real episodes from reset, with the
891
      128-step cap. Survival is the fraction reaching the cap. Walls passed counts walls leaving the

892
      and imagined-training runs advance their random keys under different iteration schedules; their
893
      ﬁnal evaluations are not paired episode trials. Policy seeds are separate runs conditional on a ﬁtted
894   model; independently ﬁtted encoders provide a different replication level. The ﬂow model has only

899   ment. A white vertical wall of width three pixels spawns at x = 60 every 22 steps and moves left
900   three pixels per step. Its 16-pixel gap is sampled at spawn, top or bottom with equal probability, and
901   becomes visible when the wall reaches x ≤ 40. The gap location is hidden state before that reveal.
902   Border or wall contact produces reward −1 and termination; the time cap is 128 steps.

904   Latent diagnostic. For each encoder, the diagnostic uses 64 real contexts with the wall still solid
905   at x ∈ {42, 45}. Reconstructed simulator states generate recorded futures and matched wall-free
906   futures under the recorded actions. The recorded branch survives the diagnostic horizon by con-
907   struction. At each step, wall amplitude is the squared Euclidean distance between encodings of the
908
      wall-present and wall-absent frames. The teacher-forced residual uses real encoded history and is a

909

910
