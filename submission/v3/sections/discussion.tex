\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Two mechanisms, two places to look.}
The representation mechanism shows up before any rollout: a position read-out on \emph{real}
encodings fails, decoded real frames lose the cube, and no change to the predictor or planner can
bring it back. The planning mechanism shows up only after the state has been kept: the gate latent
supports control from real transitions but its imagination does not. A failed imagination policy
should first be assigned to one of the two, because the remedies are disjoint: changing what the
encoder is asked to keep addresses the first; distributional or value-aware prediction addresses the
second.

\paragraph{What the flow reference does and does not show.}
The flow model differs from the JEPA in supervision stage as well as in sampling, so its advantage
is a recipe-level result. The ordering claim survives the caveat: a sampled predictor cannot restore
a state the encoder never kept, and where the JEPA held the state the remaining gap is the kind
sampling addresses. Generative modeling is not claimed to be necessary; for a quadratic goal cost a
mean and a variance suffice, and value-equivalent models can carry the needed expectations directly.

\paragraph{Boundaries.}
The per-frame-noise-plus-wind failure has high prediction loss and is not the zero-loss nuisance
optimum of Proposition~\ref{prop:representation}, which explains why non-collapse cannot protect
the state, not which finite-budget model drops it. Every behavioral result rests on one fitted model
per family and world, the gate JEPA on a preselected seed, and every decoded-latent result on a
supervised decoder. The worlds are small by design; Craftax shows the consequence at the scale of a
game under a fixed ten-minute budget, not at scale.

\paragraph{Outlook.}
General world models need a principle for choosing what to keep, and predictability is not it.
The results here argue for objectives that price the controlled state's innovations as
information to model rather than as loss to avoid, and for evaluating a learned world model at
three separate points: what its representation retains, what its predictions preserve, and what a
policy trained inside it can do.
