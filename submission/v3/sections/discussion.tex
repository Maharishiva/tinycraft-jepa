\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Two mechanisms, two remedies.}
The representation mechanism shows up before any rollout, in a read-out on \emph{real} encodings,
and no change to the predictor or planner can bring the state back; the planning mechanism shows up
only after the state has been kept. Changing what the encoder is asked to keep addresses the first;
distributional or value-aware prediction addresses the second, and generative modeling is not
claimed to be necessary: for a quadratic goal cost a mean and a variance suffice.

\paragraph{Boundaries.}
The flow model differs from the JEPA in supervision stage as well as in sampling, so its advantage
is a recipe-level result; the ordering claim survives, since a sampled predictor cannot restore a
state the encoder never kept. The per-frame-noise-plus-wind failure has high prediction loss and is
not the zero-loss optimum of Proposition~\ref{prop:representation}, which explains why non-collapse
cannot protect the state, not which finite-budget model drops it. Every behavioral result rests on
one fitted model per family and world, the gate JEPA on a preselected seed, and every decoded-latent
result on a supervised decoder.

\paragraph{Outlook.}
General world models need a principle for choosing what to keep, and predictability is not it.
The results argue for objectives that price the controlled state's innovations as information to
model rather than loss to avoid, and for evaluating a world model at three points: what its
representation retains, what its predictions preserve, and what a policy trained inside it can do.
