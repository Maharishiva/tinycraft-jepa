      1   I NTRODUCTION
039
040
      A world model is supposed to discard what does not matter. Joint-embedding predictive architec-
041
      tures (JEPAs) make this a learning problem: an encoder and a predictor are trained together so that
042   the predictor forecasts the encoder’s own next output, and nothing in the objective asks for pixels
043   back (LeCun, 2022; Assran et al., 2023; Bardes et al., 2024; Maes et al., 2026). The promise for
044   physical intelligence is a compact, reusable description of the world, free of texture and lighting.
045   The difﬁculty is that the same objective decides two things at once: what to encode and how to pre-
046   dict it. We show that in stochastic worlds it can decide both against the controller, for two reasons
047   that are independent of each other and fail in different places.
048   The ﬁrst reason is representational. Squared next-embedding error is minimized by keeping pre-
049   dictable features and dropping unpredictable ones. A background ﬁxed for an episode is perfectly
050   predictable even if the object that the agent controls is ignored (Sobal et al., 2022). Less obviously,
051   the controlled object itself becomes the unpredictable part as soon as its dynamics carry fresh inno-
052   vations that no history reveals. The objective then has a reason to learn to ignore what matters, and
053   it does so without collapsing: the latent stays non-degenerate, the Gaussian regularizer is satisﬁed
      and the prediction loss is low.

      The second reason is predictive. A squared loss produces the conditional mean of the next latent.
055   Two exact calculations show what a planner loses by working with that mean even when it is correct.
056   An action whose outcomes straddle the goal has its mean exactly on the goal, so a planner scoring
057   the mean prefers it to an action that reliably lands close. And an exact one-step mean, applied
058   recursively, need not equal the multi-step mean whenever the dynamics are nonlinear. Neither failure
059   is an optimization failure, and no better estimate of the same mean repairs it.
060
      We make both mechanisms measurable in a deliberately small visual world: one red cube, four
061   movement actions, a background that is either redrawn every frame or held for the episode, and
062   optional stochastic wind or an approaching wall whose opening is revealed late. Position, goal and
063   executed path are directly visible, so representation and planning can be separated without leaning
064   on an aggregate game score. The main empirical ﬁndings are:

      What repairs mechanism 2, and what does not. A model that predicts expected costs or values
163   directly can keep the needed expectations without samples (Wan et al., 2019; Grimm et al., 2021); for
164   the quadratic cost in Equation 2 a mean plus one scalar variance per action sufﬁces. A distributional
165   world model, such as a ﬂow-matching predictor that samples Z ′ , is the more general repair: it keeps
166   distinct outcomes and averages their costs. Neither repair touches mechanism 1. If the encoder has
167   already dropped the state, a perfect distribution over the remaining features is a perfect distribution
168   over nothing useful. This ordering is the paper’s central prediction, and Sections 5 and 6 test it with
169   a ﬂow model trained on the same data and budget as the JEPA.

171   3   W ORLDS , MODELS AND THE COMPARISON CONTRACT
172
173   The red-cube worlds. A red 6 × 6 cube moves in a 63 × 63 RGB frame. Four actions move
174   it three pixels left, up, right or down. Touching the border ends the episode with reward −1; all
175   other rewards are zero and episodes are capped at 128 steps. The background is gray noise, either
176   redrawn every frame (per-frame) or drawn once per episode (persistent). Optional wind is a hidden
177   two-dimensional random walk: each coordinate moves by −1, 0, +1 with probabilities 0.1, 0.8, 0.1,
178   is clipped to [−2, 2] and is added to the action displacement. Wind is never observed, so even a
179   perfect history leaves fresh uncertainty about the next position. Crossing the four gray conditions
180   with deterministic or wind dynamics gives the matched study of Section 4.
181   The gate world adds a wall that approaches the cube with a single opening at the top or the bottom,
182   sampled at spawn and revealed only after a ﬁxed delay. Surviving requires retreating, observing the
183   opening and then committing. The solid part of the wall is common to both futures, so a model that
184   keeps state but averages over the two openings should still show the wall; a model that loses the wall
185   has lost state, not just resolved uncertainty in the wrong way.
186
187   The JEPA. The encoder produces zt ∈ R512 arranged as a 4 × 4 × 32 grid; a factored spatial-
188   temporal predictor conditions on eight past latents and actions. Encoder and predictor are trained
189
      jointly with Equation 1, λ = 0.09, and nothing else. Each model sees 500,000 uniformly random
      transitions for 8,500 updates, about ten minutes on one A100. Three independent model seeds are
190
      ﬁtted in the matched nuisance study. Pixel decoders, position read-outs and reward or termination
191
      heads are ﬁtted afterwards on the frozen latent; their labels and compute are additional and are
192   reported as such.
193
194   The distributional reference. The ﬂow recipe uses a reconstruction-trained tokenizer with the
195   same 4 × 4 × 32 latent shape and a causal ﬂow-matching predictor that samples the next latent
196   (Lipman et al., 2023; Hafner et al., 2025). It is trained on the same transitions under the same wall-
197   clock budget, with reward and termination heads ﬁtted jointly. The two recipes differ in more than
198   deterministic versus sampled prediction: the ﬂow tokenizer is supervised by reconstruction, and its
199   task heads send gradients into its dynamics trunk, whereas the JEPA is reward-free with post-hoc
200   heads. Every JEPA-versus-ﬂow gap in this paper is therefore a recipe gap. We narrow it where we
201   can, by reﬁtting the JEPA heads on all 500,000 training labels, and we say so where we cannot.
202
203   Behavioral comparison contract. Policies are compared through one interface only. Both fami-
204
      lies expose decoded RGB frames from their own frozen decoders to the same pixel CNN and GRU
      policy, trained by the same PPO implementation with the same optimizer, updates, batch and hori-
205
      zon, and evaluated with the ﬁnal checkpoint on 256 stored episodes whose reset, texture and action
206
      keys are shared across families and seeds. Episodes are admitted by surviving a seven-step random
207   preﬁx, never by policy outcome. Before any imagination result is read, the same policy is trained on
208   real transitions observed through each model’s decoder; a family whose decoded-real control fails
209   has an interface problem, not an imagination problem. Rewards and terminations inside imagination
210   come from each model’s own heads; no simulator quantity enters training.
211
212   Craftax. Craftax-Classic (Matthews et al., 2024) is the consequence outside the toy: 17 actions,
213   procedurally generated maps, sparse rewards for 22 achievements. Both world models are trained
214   on the same 500,000-transition archive under the same ten-minute budget, and the same pixel policy
215   is trained inside each for the same number of imagined steps before native evaluation (Section 6).
      Complete settings are in Appendix D.

      detail (Assran et al., 2023; Bardes et al., 2024). LeWorldModel jointly learns encoder and action-
      conditioned predictor with a squared loss and a Gaussian regularizer (Maes et al., 2026; Balestriero
404
      & LeCun, 2025) and is the recipe we study. DINO-WM and V-JEPA 2 predict on frozen pretrained
405
      features (Zhou et al., 2024; Assran et al., 2025); PLDM learns the representation jointly with addi-
406   tional regularizers (Sobal et al., 2025). Sobal et al. (2022) study action-conditioned moving objects
407   with ﬁxed or changing distractors and identify the predictable-nuisance failure that our Proposition 1
408   specializes to the Gaussian-regularized objective. Our matched study adds fresh, unobservable inno-
409   vations in the controlled state and ﬁnds that the effect of nuisance persistence reverses under them;
410   this is a boundary in a different recipe, not a contradiction of their ﬁndings. Gulati & Nemenman
411   (2026) examine slow-noise bias in contrastive learning.
412
413   Prediction versus planning geometry. LeWorldModel itself reports accurate probes alongside
414   weaker planning. Temporal-Distance JEPA and RC-aux add temporal or reachability structure, and
415   physically grounded variants add task-state supervision (Bai & Xiong, 2026; Li et al., 2026; Yan
416   et al., 2026; Liu et al., 2026); Terver et al. (2026) study planner and architecture choices and note
417
      multimodal conditional means. Our exact examples isolate the decision consequences of scoring at
      a mean and of composing mean transitions, independent of any architecture.
418
419
420
      Generative and distributional imagination. Generative world models have supported control in
      imagination (Hafner et al., 2020; Alonso et al., 2024; Hafner et al., 2025) and also exhibit perception,
421
      action-conditioning and rollout failures (Hansen & Wang, 2026). FlowWM learns stochastic feature-
422
      space futures without actions and reports no control (Porcher et al., 2026). Our ﬂow reference is
423   action-conditioned, trained on the JEPA’s data and budget, and compared through a shared policy so
424   that behavior, not video quality, is the endpoint.
425
426   Objective mismatch and value equivalence. That a model good for prediction need not be good
427   for control is longstanding (Lambert et al., 2020). Value-aware model learning, expectation models
428   and (proper) value equivalence ﬁt the quantities a decision class needs (Farahmand et al., 2017; Wan
429   et al., 2019; Grimm et al., 2020; 2021); TD-JEPA predicts policy-conditioned successor features
430   (Bagatella et al., 2026). These are the reason our mean-state examples are not impossibility results
431   for deterministic world models: a model can keep expectations without keeping samples, provided
      the representation still carries the state those expectations depend on.

      8   D ISCUSSION
433
434
      Two mechanisms, two places to look. The representation mechanism shows up before any rollout:
435   a position read-out on real encodings fails, decoded real frames lose the cube, and no change to
436   the predictor or planner can bring it back. The planning mechanism shows up only after the state
437   has been kept: the gate latent supports control from real transitions but its imagination does not. A
438   practitioner who sees a failed imagination policy should ﬁrst check which of the two they are looking
439   at, because the remedies are disjoint. Detaching the target, adding decoders, or changing what the
440   encoder is asked to keep addresses the ﬁrst; distributional or value-aware prediction addresses the
441   second.
442
443   What the ﬂow reference does and does not show. The ﬂow model differs from the JEPA in
444   supervision stage, not only in sampling versus point prediction, so its advantage where it has one is
445   a recipe-level result. The ordering claim survives this caveat: a sampled predictor cannot restore a
446
      state that the encoder never kept, and where the JEPA representation held the state, the remaining
      gap is the kind that sampling addresses. We do not claim that generative modeling is necessary; for a
447
      quadratic goal cost a mean and a variance sufﬁce, and value-equivalent models can carry the needed
448
      expectations directly.
449
450
      Honest boundaries. The empirical per-frame-noise-plus-wind failure has high prediction loss and
451
      is not an instance of the zero-loss nuisance optimum of Proposition 1; the proposition explains why
452   non-collapse cannot protect the state, not which ﬁnite-budget model drops it. The gate JEPA encoder
453   used for the behavioral comparison is model seed 1, chosen because it was the only seed on which
454   decoded controls had succeeded in development, and every ﬂow gate policy shares one dynamics
455   checkpoint. Decoded-latent results are statements about information recoverable by a supervised
456   decoder, not about the unsupervised model alone. The worlds are small by design; Craftax shows
457   the consequence at the scale of a real game but under a ﬁxed ten-minute budget.
458
459   Outlook. General world models need a principle for choosing what to keep, and predictability is
460   not it. The results here argue for objectives that price the controlled state’s innovations as informa-
461   tion to model rather than as loss to avoid, and for evaluating a learned world model at three separate
462
      points: what its representation retains, what its predictions preserve, and what a policy trained inside
      it can do.

589
590   Write observations as xt = R(pt , b), where pt is controlled state and b is an episode-constant nui-
591   sance recoverable from the image. Assume that b is independent of states and actions in the training
592   data. At deployment, assume it is independent of the initial state and transition randomness, and does
593   not directly affect dynamics or reward. Let SIGReg∞ be the nonnegative population characteristic-
      function discrepancy, which vanishes for N (0, ID ) (Appendix B).

594
      Proposition 1 (Gaussian nuisance optimum; extending Sobal et al. (2022)). If the encoder class
595   contains a map f (R(p, b)) = φ(b) with φ(b) ∼ N (0, ID ) and the predictor class contains the last-
596   latent copy map, their population prediction loss and SIGReg∞ are both zero. This is a global opti-
597   mum with covariance ID and I(zt ; p1:T ) = 0 under the training distribution. A deployed controller
598   observing only these latents, time, and its own actions has the value of a randomized state-blind
599   policy.
600
      The argument is simple: nuisance changes across episodes, satisfying the marginal constraint, but
601
      is constant within each episode, making prediction exact. Its deployment value follows by giving
602
      a state-blind policy the same private nuisance draw and controller randomness. The Gaussianiza-
603   tion assumption requires a rich nuisance and a realizable measurable map; a ﬁnite texture does not
604   literally instantiate it. The ﬁnite-batch regularizer also differs from the population functional. A com-
605   plementary innovation bound in Appendix B shows that an encoder preserving all state distances at
606   a ﬁxed scale must incur prediction error for unpredictable state innovations.
